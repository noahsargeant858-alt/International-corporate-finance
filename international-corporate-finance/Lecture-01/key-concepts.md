## Core Financial Models (You Will Calculate All of These)

### WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital
**What it is:** The average rate a company pays to finance its assets, weighted by the proportion of debt and equity used.

**Simple version:** If a company is 60% funded by equity costing 10% and 40% funded by debt costing 5% (after tax), the WACC is (0.6 × 10%) + (0.4 × 5%) = 8%. This 8% is the minimum return any investment must earn to add value.

**Why it matters:** It's the "hurdle rate" — investments that return less than WACC destroy company value.

---

### CAPM — Capital Asset Pricing Model
**What it is:** A formula that tells you the return investors require on an equity investment given its level of market risk.

**Formula:** `Expected Return = Risk-Free Rate + Beta × (Market Return − Risk-Free Rate)`

- **Risk-Free Rate** — return on government bonds (e.g. UK gilts), considered zero-risk
- **Beta (β)** — how much the stock moves relative to the market (β > 1 = more volatile than market)
- **Market Risk Premium** — the extra return the market gives above the risk-free rate

**Why it matters:** Used to calculate the cost of equity component in WACC.

---

### APM — Arbitrage Pricing Model
**What it is:** A more sophisticated alternative to CAPM that uses multiple risk factors rather than just one (market risk).

**Simple version:** Instead of saying "risk = how it moves with the market," APM says "risk = sensitivity to inflation + sensitivity to GDP growth + sensitivity to interest rates..." etc.

**Why it matters:** More realistic than CAPM for international companies exposed to multiple macro risks.

---

### NPV — Net Present Value
**What it is:** The value today of all future cash flows from an investment, minus the initial cost.

**Decision rule:** NPV > 0 = invest. NPV < 0 = reject. Highest NPV = best project.

**Why it matters:** Gold standard of investment appraisal. Accounts for the time value of money (£1 today is worth more than £1 in 5 years).

---

### IRR — Internal Rate of Return
**What it is:** The discount rate at which an investment's NPV equals zero — essentially the percentage return the project generates.

**Decision rule:** IRR > cost of capital (WACC) = invest. IRR < cost of capital = reject.

**Why it matters:** Gives a percentage figure that's easy to compare against borrowing costs.

---

### ARR — Accounting Rate of Return
**What it is:** Average annual profit from an investment expressed as a percentage of the initial investment.

**Formula:** `ARR = (Average Annual Profit / Initial Investment) × 100`

**Decision rule:** Higher ARR = better. Compare against a target rate.

**Limitation:** Ignores time value of money (treats £1 in year 1 the same as £1 in year 10).

---

### Payback Period
**What it is:** How long it takes to recover the initial investment from cash inflows.

**Decision rule:** Shorter payback = better. Often used alongside NPV/IRR.

**Limitation:** Ignores everything that happens after the payback point and ignores time value of money.

## Sustainability Concepts

### Elkington's Triple Bottom Line (TBL)
**What it is:** A framework suggesting companies should measure success on three dimensions, not just profit.

| Bottom Line | Measures |
|-------------|---------|
| **People** | Social impact — employee welfare, community, supply chain ethics |
| **Planet** | Environmental impact — carbon emissions, resource use, pollution |
| **Profit** | Economic performance — the traditional financial bottom line |

**Why it matters:** Oil companies face pressure from investors, regulators, and the public to account for all three. Purely financial decisions that ignore Planet/People are increasingly unacceptable.

---

### IFRS S1 & S2 (Sustainability Reporting Standards)
**What they are:** International Financial Reporting Standards specifically for sustainability disclosures.

- **IFRS S1** — General requirements: companies must disclose how sustainability risks and opportunities affect them financially
- **IFRS S2** — Climate-specific: detailed disclosure of climate-related risks, emissions data, transition plans

**Why it matters:** Companies (especially oil firms) increasingly must quantify and report their climate exposure — this feeds directly into investment decisions.

## International Trade Terms (Incoterms)

### CFR — Cost and Freight
**What it is:** The seller pays for the goods and the freight to get them to the named destination port. Insurance is NOT included — the buyer has to arrange and pay for it separately.

**Risk transfer point:** The moment the goods are loaded onto the ship at the origin port — even though the seller is still paying for the voyage.

### CIF — Cost, Insurance and Freight
**What it is:** Same as CFR, but the seller also buys marine insurance and hands the buyer the policy.

**Risk transfer point:** Same as CFR — loading at the origin port. The seller has just pre-arranged insurance on the buyer's behalf; it doesn't shift when risk passes.

**The trap:** Cost transfer and risk transfer are not the same moment. Under both terms, if the cargo is lost at sea after loading, it's legally the buyer's loss — the difference is only whether the buyer already has an insurance policy in hand (CIF) or has to sort their own out (CFR).

**Why it matters for Activity I:** Russia and the USA quote CFR (add 3% insurance yourself). Netherlands quotes CIF (insurance already priced in). You can't compare the three sticker prices directly — add insurance to Russia/USA first, or you'll under-cost them relative to the Dutch quote.

### Documentary Payment Methods (also relevant to Activity I)
| Method | How it works | Risk to Seller |
|--------|-------------|----------------|
| **Open Account** | Buyer pays after an agreed period, no bank guarantee | Highest — seller trusts buyer to pay |
| **D/A Bill of Exchange** | Bank releases shipping documents once buyer accepts to pay later | Medium — buyer could still default |
| **Confirmed Irrevocable LC** | Bank guarantees payment regardless of buyer's situation | Lowest — bank carries the risk |
