## Your Case Study: An Oil Company at a Crossroads

The module case study is built around an oil company facing the defining corporate finance challenge of the 2020s: **how do you remain financially viable while transitioning away from the thing that made you profitable?**

This isn't fictional drama — it's exactly what BP, Shell, ExxonMobil and Saudi Aramco are grappling with right now.

---

## Real-World Parallel: BP's Strategic Pivot

**The situation:** In 2020, BP announced it would reduce oil production by 40% by 2030 and invest $5bn/year in renewables. The rationale was the energy transition. The problem? Shareholders revolted — BP's stock underperformed ExxonMobil (which doubled down on oil) for three years.

**The corporate finance questions this raises:**
- What's the **NPV** of investing in solar farms vs continued oil extraction?
- How does BP **raise capital** (WACC) when its future earnings are uncertain?
- What **risk premium** (CAPM/APM) do investors demand for a company in transition?
- Is acquiring a renewables company (M&A) better than building capacity from scratch?

**The outcome:** BP walked back parts of its green strategy in 2023 under investor pressure — a classic case of Profit winning over Planet in the short term.

---

## Real-World Parallel: Shell's WACC Problem

Shell funds itself through a mix of equity (shareholders) and debt (bondholders). As the energy transition accelerates:

- **Equity investors** demand higher returns (higher Beta — more uncertainty)
- **Green bond** investors offer cheaper debt — but only for qualifying green projects
- **Traditional bondholders** want higher rates to compensate for stranded asset risk

Result: Shell's WACC is becoming more complex to calculate and manage. This is exactly the kind of analysis LO2 asks you to do.

## Real-World Parallel: Investment Appraisal in Action (LO4)

**Scenario:** An oil company is deciding whether to invest £500m in a new North Sea oil field vs £500m in an offshore wind farm.

| | Oil Field | Wind Farm |
|--|-----------|-----------|
| Payback | 4 years | 7 years |
| ARR | 18% | 12% |
| NPV (at 8% WACC) | £120m | £85m |
| IRR | 22% | 16% |

**Pure finance says:** invest in the oil field — it wins on every metric.

**But:** Factor in carbon pricing risk, ESG investor pressure, potential regulatory bans on new oil fields by 2030, and the wind farm becomes more attractive on a risk-adjusted basis.

This tension — between the numbers and the strategic context — is exactly what your case study will ask you to navigate.

## Real-World M&A Example (LO5)

**ExxonMobil + Pioneer Natural Resources (2023) — $60bn deal**

- Why: Acquire shale oil reserves cheaply as Pioneer's valuation dipped
- Risk: Overpaying (winner's curse), integration complexity, regulatory scrutiny
- Outcome: Massively boosted Exxon's production capacity but attracted ESG criticism

**What you'll do in LO5:** Value a potential acquisition target using financial models, identify synergies (cost savings + revenue boosts), and flag the risks — exactly as an investment bank analyst would.

## Why "International" Corporate Finance?

The "international" prefix adds these layers of complexity:

| Domestic CF | International CF |
|-------------|-----------------|
| One currency | Multiple currencies → **exchange rate risk** |
| One legal system | Multiple jurisdictions → **political/regulatory risk** |
| One interest rate | Different rates globally → **interest rate risk** |
| Home market knowledge | Foreign market uncertainty → **information asymmetry** |

**Example:** Your oil company invests in a field in Nigeria. The project's NPV is calculated in US dollars (oil is priced in USD). But you're a UK company reporting in GBP. A 10% dollar weakening wipes out a significant chunk of your projected returns — even if the oil field itself performs perfectly.

This is why WACC, NPV and IRR calculations in international finance need to account for exchange rate movements — which is what your real-time data updates throughout the semester will test.

## The Sustainability Reporting Angle You'll Need

**IFRS S1 & S2 — Why They Matter to Your Case Study**

From 2024-2026, large companies in the UK and EU are being required to publish climate disclosures. For an oil company, this means:

- Publishing **Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions** data
- Showing a credible **net zero transition plan**
- Disclosing **climate-related financial risks** (e.g. what happens to asset values if carbon tax hits £150/tonne)

For your case study analysis: sustainability reporting isn't just a legal box-tick — it affects the company's **cost of capital** (ESG investors demand it), its **M&A attractiveness** (buyers scrutinise it), and its **long-term cash flow forecasts** (carbon costs reduce future profits).

Connect your LO1–LO5 analysis back to these sustainability themes and you'll demonstrate exactly the kind of integrated thinking the module rewards.
