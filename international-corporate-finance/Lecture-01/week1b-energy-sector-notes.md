## 1. Industry Structure: Upstream, Midstream & Downstream

The oil industry is split into three segments. UK Oil Plc is an **upstream** company.

| Segment | What It Does | Example Activities |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Upstream** | Find and extract crude oil | Geological surveys, drilling, production |
| **Midstream** | Transport and store crude | Pipelines, tankers, storage terminals |
| **Downstream** | Refine and sell products | Refineries, petrol stations, petrochemicals |

**For your case study:** UK Oil extracts crude from the North Sea → sells to refineries downstream. Activity I (materials supply) and Activity J (platform construction) are the key upstream cost drivers.

## 2. The Four Phases of Upstream Operations

### Phase 1: Acquisition
Buying the **right to explore** from a national government (licence). This is a legal/financial step before a single drill touches the seabed.

> **In your case study:** UK Oil has already spent £50M acquiring drilling rights and £20M on geological studies (Activity A). Both are **sunk costs** — they do NOT appear in your NPV calculation (money already spent, can't be recovered).

### Phase 2: Exploration & Evaluation
Seismic surveys and test drilling to determine if commercially viable oil actually exists.

**Key risk:** You can spend hundreds of millions and find nothing.

> **Real example:** Shell spent **$7 billion** exploring the Arctic. In 2015 they stopped — the well was "disappointing." That $7bn was written off. This is the dry hole risk you must factor into your evaluation of Option 1.

### Phase 3: Development & Production
Building the infrastructure (platform, pipelines, processing facilities) and drilling production wells.

**Critical characteristics:**
- **Lead times of 10+ years** from licence to first sale — massive cash outflows long before any revenue
- **Long-term contracts** often needed to justify infrastructure investment
- **Cross-border pipelines** create political risk (think Russia/Ukraine gas disputes)
- **Environmental liability** is enormous (BP's Deepwater Horizon cost $65 billion in fines and cleanup)

> **In your case study:** Activities F–K cover this phase. Activity J (oil platform) alone is £300M (British supplier) or €355M (German supplier). You'll need to choose.

### Phase 4: Refinery (Downstream)
Crude is refined into sellable products. **UK Oil doesn't do this** — they sell crude to refineries.

**The Crack Spread:** The margin between crude oil price and refined product prices. Volatile — affects downstream companies' willingness to pay UK Oil for crude.

## 3. Resources vs Reserves — Critical Distinction

| Term | Definition | Financial Significance |
|------|-----------|----------------------|
| **Resources** | Oil estimated to be present in the ground (may or may not be extractable) | Cannot be used as collateral |
| **Reserves** | Resources that are commercially recoverable with current technology at current prices | **Bankable** — used as basis for borrowing and equity valuation |

**Why reserves matter to UK Oil's financing:**
- Lenders use proven reserves as security for loans
- Equity investors price the company based on its reserve base
- If the new North Sea field has low/uncertain reserves, financing becomes harder and more expensive

---

## 4. Reserve Replacement Ratio (RRR)

$$RRR = \frac{\text{Extensions \& Discoveries} + \text{Improved Recoveries}}{\text{Production}}$$

- **RRR = 100%:** You replaced exactly what you produced — treading water
- **RRR > 100%:** Growing your reserve base — good
- **RRR < 100%:** Depleting faster than you're replacing — existential problem

> **BP example:** In 2012 BP's RRR was only **6%** (excluding TNK-BP) — one of the consequences of the Deepwater Horizon disaster diverting focus away from exploration. A company that consistently can't replace its reserves will run out of oil.

**For your case study:** Option 1 (new North Sea field) can be framed as a strategic necessity to maintain UK Oil's RRR. This is a non-financial justification for the financial investment.

## 5. The Big Risks in the Oil Sector

### Financial Risks
| Risk | Description | Relevance to Case Study |
|------|-------------|------------------------|
| **Oil price volatility** | Brent crude went from $100 in Sept 2014 to $50 in Jan 2015 | Revenue projections in your cash flow model must account for this |
| **Exchange rate risk** | Oil is priced in USD; UK Oil reports in GBP | German platform (€355M) and Russian/US/Dutch material suppliers all involve FX risk |
| **Interest rate risk** | Floating rate loans = costs rise if rates rise | UK Oil's existing £200M floating rate loan; any new loan |
| **Capital intensity** | Massive upfront CAPEX before any revenue | Long payback periods; NPV sensitive to discount rate |

### Operational Risks
- **Dry hole risk** — exploration finds nothing (Shell Arctic = $7bn lost)
- **Technical failure** — platform accidents, pipeline leaks
- **Supply chain disruption** — Activity G shortage of safety engineers
- **Political risk** — cross-border pipelines, foreign acquisitions (Abu Dhabi remittance restrictions)

### Strategic Risks
- **Finite resource** — oil is not renewable; must continually replace reserves
- **Energy transition** — demand could fall as renewables grow
- **Environmental regulation** — carbon taxes, drilling bans, liability
- **"Big Elephants" are gone** — the largest, cheapest oil fields have already been found; new finds are smaller, deeper, more expensive

## 6. Porter's Five Forces in the Oil Sector

Understanding competitive forces helps justify strategic decisions in your report.

| Force | Situation in Oil Sector |
|-------|------------------------|
| **Threat of New Entrants** | Low — massive capital requirements create barriers |
| **Supplier Power** | High for specialist equipment (e.g. only 2 platform suppliers for UK Oil) |
| **Buyer Power** | Moderate — refineries have alternatives but crude is commoditised |
| **Threat of Substitutes** | Growing — renewables increasing; EV adoption reducing demand |
| **Industry Rivalry** | High — global competition, price set by market |

## 7. The Saudi Dilemma — A Model for Understanding UK Oil

Saudi Arabia earns 73% of GDP from oil. Their strategic problem is a concentrated version of every oil company's problem:

- Need capital to replace oil reserves
- Need capital to invest in alternative energy
- Need capital for domestic development
- But low oil prices reduce the capital available

**UK Oil faces the same tension at company level.** This is why the Board's decision — North Sea expansion vs Abu Dhabi M&A — is genuinely difficult, and why your recommendation must account for risk, return, and long-term strategic positioning.
