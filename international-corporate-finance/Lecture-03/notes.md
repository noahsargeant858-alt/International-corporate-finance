## 1. Introduction — Where Finance Sits on the Balance Sheet

> **Investment Decisions = Finance Decisions** (two sides of the same balance sheet)

```
Non-Current Assets            Equity
      +                          +
Current Assets             Non-Current Liabilities
                                  +
                            Current Liabilities
```

The left side (assets) is what you **own** — the investment decision. The right side (equity + liabilities) is what you **owe** — the finance decision. Every pound of CAPEX on the left has to be funded by something on the right.

### Long-term vs short-term sources

| | **Long-Term** (repayable > 1 year) | **Short-Term** (repayable within 1 year) |
|---|---|---|
| **Funds** | CAPEX — PPE, exploration, M&A, alternative energy | OPEX — operating cash-flow shortages while waiting for sales income |
| **Equity sources** | Ordinary, Preference, Cumulative Preference, Redeemable shares | — |
| **Debt sources** | Loans, Debentures, Bonds, Convertible Bonds, Leasing | Overdrafts, Accounts Payable, Invoice Discounting, Factoring, Bills of Exchange |

### Which one applies to your coursework

> **OPEX:** any operational deficit is covered by short-term finance or the company's other income (not this project) — **you can ignore short-term finance for the coursework.**

> **CAPEX (up to £350M):** finance will be raised specifically for this project — the company will not have this sitting in the bank. **You are looking at long-term sources only: Equity and/or Debt.**

## 2. Equity

### Types of share capital
Ordinary · Preference · Cumulative Preference · Convertible Preference · Redeemable

### IPO — Initial Public Offering
A private company (funded by founders, family, friends, venture capitalists or private equity) has limited ability to grow. An **IPO** offers shares to the public via the **Primary Market**, raising new capital directly for the company.

**Worked example — Microsoft's 1986 IPO:**
- IPO price: $21/share, 2.5M shares traded on day one
- Market cap on listing: 2,500,000 × $21 = **$52,500,000** (raised via the **Primary Market** — cash goes to Microsoft)
- Closed day one at $27.75 (+32.14%) → market cap **$69,375,000**
- Anyone who bought and sold that day traded on the **Secondary Market** — Microsoft is not involved, receives no cash from this trade, and simply has a new shareholder

**Why the Primary/Secondary distinction matters:** a company only raises cash when it *issues* shares (Primary Market). Every trade afterwards (Secondary Market) moves the share between two investors — it doesn't give the company a penny, but the company still carries a permanent dividend obligation to whoever ends up holding the shares.

### Rights Issue — UK Oil is already listed, so this is the equity route that applies

A Rights Issue gives **existing shareholders** the right to buy additional shares, in proportion to their existing holding — this is how an already-listed company (like UK Oil) raises new equity, as opposed to an IPO (only relevant to a company going public for the first time).

### Full worked example (reproduce this method for your coursework)

**Setup:** Company needs to raise 200,000 for a new project. Issued share capital = 500,000 × £1 ordinary shares. Makes a **1-for-5 Rights Issue at £2/share.**

```
Issued Share Capital:        500,000 × £1 ordinary shares
Rights Issue (1 new for 5):  100,000 × £1 ordinary shares
Total now in issue:          600,000 × £1 ordinary shares

Current Market Price/Share  = £2.50
Right to Buy (the discount) = £2.00
VALUE OF THE RIGHT           = £0.50

Nominal/Face Value           = £1.00
Issue Price                  = £2.00
Share Premium = £1.00 × 100,000 shares = £100,000

Cash Received = 100,000 new shares × £2 = £200,000
```

That £200,000 cash increases the company's Net Worth / Book Value directly.

### Effect on Book Value per Share

```
Pre-issue:  Book Value/Net Worth 700,000 ÷ 500,000 shares = £1.40/share
Post-issue: Book Value/Net Worth 900,000 ÷ 600,000 shares = £1.50/share
```

> ⚠️ **"Why don't they just use Retained Profit instead?"** — **Retained profit is NOT cash.** It's a record of what the company already owes its shareholders (profit earned but not yet distributed) — the actual cash from that profit has typically already been spent buying assets. You cannot raise new finance from a number that just represents what you already owe. This trips people up constantly — if a question presents retained profit as a "free" source of new cash, that's the error to spot.

### Shareholder's choice when a Rights Issue is announced

Existing shareholders have the **right but not the obligation** to buy. Three options:

1. **Exercise the right** (buy the shares) — ownership % stays the same
2. **Renounce and sell the right** on the market (the right itself has value — in the example above, £0.50) — a buyer might pay you £0.30 for the right, then pay £2.00 to the company for the share
3. **Do nothing** — by Stock Exchange rules, the rights are normally sold on your behalf

### Dilution — the consequence of not exercising

**Worked example:** You own 5% of the 500,000 shares (= 25,000 shares). Company does a 1-for-5 rights issue (100,000 new shares).

| | If you exercise | If you don't |
|---|---|---|
| Your shares | 30,000 | 25,000 |
| Company total | 600,000 | 600,000 |
| **Your %** | **5% (unchanged)** | **25,000/600,000 = 4.16% — DILUTED** |
| Book value of your stake | 30,000 × £1.50 = £45,000 | 25,000 × £1.50 = £37,500 |

### Theoretical Ex-Rights Price (TERP)

```
TERP = (N × cum-rights price + issue price) ÷ (N + 1)
```
Where **N** = number of existing shares needed to buy one new share.

In theory, this is a mechanical adjustment to allow for the discounted new shares entering the market. **In practice**, the actual market price depends on whether the market believes the new investment will actually work — the formula gives you a benchmark, not a guarantee.

### Advantages of a Rights Issue (over a public offer)

- Cheaper than offering shares to the general public
- No prospectus required (if the issue is under 10% of the share class)
- Simpler administration, lower underwriting cost
- Shares issued at a discount — attractive to existing investors
- You can sell your right rather than buy, if you don't want to invest more
- **Relative voting rights are unaffected if all shareholders take up their rights**
- Finance raised can pay down debt — reducing gearing in both book and market value terms

## 3. Debt

### The major forms
**A.** Loans & Debentures **B.** Bonds **C.** Leasing *(not considered for this coursework)*

### Loans & Bonds for exporters/importers (relevant given your USD/EUR exposure)

A UK company can borrow in **sterling or foreign currency** (e.g. $, ¥, €) rather than automatically borrowing £.

**Eurobond — a commonly misunderstood term:**
> A Eurobond is **NOT** a bond denominated in €. It's a bond issued in a currency **foreign to the country it's issued in.** The name predates the euro — it comes from early bonds first issued in Europe. E.g. a **$ bond issued in the UK** = a Eurobond. A **£ bond issued in Germany** = also a Eurobond.

### Why might a UK company borrow $ rather than £?

1. They may need to **pay for goods in $** — borrowing in $ eliminates the exchange-rate mismatch on that specific payment
2. **To eliminate exchange rate risk** on a $-denominated obligation
3. The **interest rate on a currency loan may be lower** than an equivalent £ loan

> ⚠️ **But if you borrow $, you must repay $** — so you've swapped one FX exposure for another, unless the loan exists specifically to offset a $ cost you already have (natural hedge). This is exactly the kind of point the module wants you to evaluate, not just list.

### Bonds

A bond is often described as an **unsecured I.O.U.** Companies and governments issue bonds to raise long-term finance as an alternative to:
- issuing shares (gives away ownership), or
- a loan/debenture (may require security and restrictive covenants)

Bonds are typically traded on the **Secondary Market** after issue.

**Key terms (worked example: £100M 5% AAA-rated Bond 2030):**
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Coupon Rate** | 5% — the interest paid per year |
| **Redemption Date** | 2030 — when the £100M capital is repaid |
| **Credit Rating** | AAA |
| **Par value** | £100M (the face value) |
| **Above Par** | Sold for *more* than face value — attractive coupon (e.g. £102M for this bond) |
| **Below Par** | Sold for *less* than face value — unattractive coupon (e.g. £98M) |
| **Zero Coupon Bond** | 0% interest, sold at a discount — e.g. pay £90M now, receive £100M in 2030 *if the company hasn't gone into liquidation* |

### Credit Ratings

Rated by agencies: **Standard & Poor's, Moody's, Fitch.**

| Rating band | Description | 31-yr average default rate |
|---|---|---|
| AAA | Prime | 0% |
| AA | High grade | 0.38% |
| A | Upper medium grade | 0.39% |
| BBB | Lower medium grade | 1.02% |
| BB | Non-investment grade / speculative | 4.02% |
| B | Highly speculative | 13.84% |
| CCC | Substantial risks | 49.28% |
| C/D | Default imminent / in default | — |

**D-rated bonds are known as Junk Bonds.** AAA-rated bonds don't need to offer a high return to attract investors — they're selling safety, not yield (provided the rating agency got it right — a pointed caveat given the 2008 financial crisis).

## 4. Financing via an SPV / SPE (Special Purpose Vehicle / Entity)

A **separate legal entity** set up specifically to finance a new project, shielding the parent/holding companies from liability.

**Typical structure:** Company + Government each contribute ~20% equity into the SPE; the SPE then raises the remaining ~60% as **debt secured on the project's own assets and revenue.**

### Why construction-phase risk matters
For a new-build project, there's **no revenue stream during construction**. Debt service only becomes possible once the project is operational — meaning all parties carry significant risk during the build phase, before any cash flow exists to service the debt.

### Recourse vs Non-Recourse Finance

| | Recourse | Non-Recourse (or Limited Recourse) |
|---|---|---|
| Lender's claim on default | Full recourse to the **shareholders'** own assets/cash flow | **No** claim beyond the SPE itself — the SPE is a genuinely separate, limited-liability entity |

### Off-Balance-Sheet Finance
Because the SPE is a separate legal entity, its debt doesn't have to appear on the parent company's own balance sheet — hence "off-balance-sheet." Banks secure a **lien** on the SPE's assets, letting them assume control of the project if the SPE breaches the loan agreement.

### Typical loan agreement terms
General conditions precedent · conditions precedent to each drawdown · availability period (with a commitment fee) · drawdown mechanics · interest clause (margin over base rate) · repayment clause · **financial covenants** (key project ratios) · dividend restrictions · representations & warranties · illegality clause.

### Advantages/Disadvantages tables (know them — do not reproduce them in your report)

**Disadvantages of Debt vs Equity:** must be repaid (shares are not bought back — they trade on to a new holder instead) · raises break-even point and insolvency risk under high leverage · floating rate exposes you to rate rises · inflexible repayment schedule (fixed principal + interest regardless of business cycle) · can restrict company activities via covenants · increases gearing, which raises perceived risk to all lenders · often requires a charge over company assets.

**Advantages of Debt vs Equity:** no dilution of ownership · lender has no claim on future profits beyond principal + interest · easy to plan for (fixed schedule, except variable-rate loans) · tax efficient (interest is tax-deductible, unlike dividends) · less regulatory complexity · less shareholder administration. Note also: because companies don't buy back shares, equity finance means a shareholder — and the dividend expectation — **forever**.

### The core gearing principle

> **The more you borrow, the higher the gearing, the higher the perceived risk of non-repayment, the higher the interest rate new lenders will demand.**

New debt taken on **now**, on top of the existing £700M, will likely be priced at a **higher rate** than the existing 6–7% debt — because adding more debt to an already-leveraged balance sheet increases risk for every lender, not just the newest one. This is the direct link into Section 5 (Cost of Capital) — a highly-geared WACC calculation isn't just an academic exercise, it reflects a real repricing risk if UK Oil takes on £300M+ in new debt for the platform.
